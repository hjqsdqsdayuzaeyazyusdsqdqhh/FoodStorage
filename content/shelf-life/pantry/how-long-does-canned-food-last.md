  - q: Is it safe to eat canned food past the expiration date?
    a: Yes, if the can is intact and has been stored in a cool, dry place. The dates on cans are best-quality guidance, not safety deadlines. Properly stored canned food stays safe for years past the date — the quality declines slowly, not the safety. Once opened, though, treat it as fresh food and refrigerate.
  - q: What does a bulging can mean?
    a: A bulging or swollen can is the single most serious warning sign in your pantry. It indicates gas produced by bacteria — including Clostridium botulinum, which causes botulism. Do not open a bulging can. Throw it in the trash without tasting, and do the same for cans that leak or spurt when opened.
  - q: Is it safe to eat food from a dented can?
    a: Usually, with one exception. Small dents that don't affect the seams are fine. A dented can is judged by location and severity — not every dent is dangerous.

Canned food is the safest thing in your pantry — and the one with the single most dangerous exception. When a can is sealed and intact, canned food lasts for years and years, outliving almost every other food you own. When a can is compromised, it can carry botulism, and that one rule is worth knowing cold.

## How long canned food lasts

| Storage method | Duration | Best practice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pantry — low-acid (vegetables, meat, soup, beans) | 2–5 years | Safe for years beyond; quality declines slowly |
| Pantry — high-acid (tomatoes, fruit, juice) | 12–18 months | Acid shortens the quality window |
| Fridge (opened) | 3–4 days | Transfer to a covered container |
| Warm storage (above 75°F) | Shorter | Heat shortens both quality and safety |
| Freezer | Never in the can | Freeze leftovers in containers, not cans |

## Storage conditions for canned food

Cans are engineered to last, but only in the right environment. Store them in a cool, dry place below 75°F — a pantry or cabinet, never over the stove, above the refrigerator, or near heat pipes. Heat degrades the food's quality and, over a long time, can compromise the can's integrity. Keep cans away from direct sunlight and off damp floors.

Rotate your stock: use the oldest cans first. The [FIFO pantry system](/pantry-organization/the-fifo-pantry-system/) makes this automatic. And wipe the tops of cans before opening — dust and grime on the lid can end up in your food.

## The botulism warning signs

Bulging. Leaking. Spouting under pressure. A bad smell or mold when opened. These are the signs of a compromised can, and they matter because one specific bacteria — Clostridium botulinum — produces a deadly toxin in sealed, low-acid cans that went wrong.

::safety
Never open a can that is bulging, swollen, leaking, or spouts liquid when pierced — it may contain botulism toxin, which is lethal even in tiny amounts and is not destroyed by cooking or boiling. Put the can in the trash unopened, and keep it away from pets and children. This rule has no exceptions.
::

## Dented cans: when to keep, when to toss

Not every dent is a problem. Cans with small, shallow dents that don't touch a seam are fine. Throw out cans with deep dents — especially on the top seam or side seams — because damage there can break the can's seal. Also discard rusty cans (rust can perforate the metal), cans that leak, and any can that bulges when you squeeze it or hisses when opened.

## Once opened, canned food becomes fresh food

The moment a can opens, its shelf life drops from years to days. Refrigerate leftovers in a covered container — not in the open can — and eat them within 3–4 days. Acidic foods like tomatoes and fruit spoil fastest once opened. Plan meals so an opened can gets used the same week.

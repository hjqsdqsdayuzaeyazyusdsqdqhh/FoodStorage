  - q: How long do potatoes last in the pantry?
    a: Potatoes keep 3–5 weeks — and sometimes up to 2–3 months — in a cool, dark, dry place like a pantry, basement, or cabinet, ideally at 45–50°F. Light and warmth cause sprouting and greening; moisture causes rot. Keep them in a paper bag or ventilated bin, never in plastic.
  - q: Should potatoes be refrigerated?
    a: No. Refrigeration converts potato starch to sugar, which makes potatoes taste sweet and turn brown faster when cooked — and it increases acrylamide formation when fried. The pantry, not the fridge, is where potatoes belong. Cold storage is for processed potato products, not fresh tubers.
  - q: Why do potatoes sprout and turn green?
    a: Sprouting and greening are responses to light and warmth. Light triggers chlorophyll (the green) and solanine (the bitter, mildly toxic compound that comes with it); warmth triggers sprouting. Store potatoes in the dark and cool to slow both, and always cut away any green parts before cooking.
  - q: Can you freeze potatoes?
    a: "Raw potatoes freeze poorly — ice crystals turn their flesh mushy and gray. But cooked potatoes freeze well: freeze mashed, roasted, or boiled potatoes for about 3 months. Frozen fries and hash browns also freeze great. For the freezer, cook first, cool, then freeze in airtight bags."
  - q: Are green or sprouted potatoes safe to eat?
    a: Cut away small sprouts and any green patches, and the rest of the potato is fine. If a potato is heavily green, shriveled, or tastes bitter, throw it out — that much solanine can cause nausea. When in doubt, discard. Sprouting itself is not a safety issue if the potato is still firm.
  - q: How long do red potatoes last?
    a: "Red potatoes follow the same rules as other potatoes: 3–5 weeks — sometimes up to 2–3 months — in a cool, dark, dry pantry, in a paper bag or ventilated bin. Potatoes are the ultimate pantry keeper, but only under the right conditions. They want what most of us don't give them: darkness, coolness, and dryness. Give them that and they'll wait for months; put them in a sunny bowl or a plastic bag and they'll sprout, green, or rot within weeks.

## How long potatoes last

| Storage method | Duration | Best practice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pantry (cool, dark, dry) | 3–5 weeks | Paper bag or ventilated bin |
| Counter (warm, light) | 1–2 weeks | Sprouts and greens faster |
| Fridge | Not recommended | Turns starch to sugar |
| Freezer (cooked) | About 3 months | Cook, cool, then freeze |

## Storage conditions for potatoes

The ideal spot is cool (45–50°F), dark, dry, and ventilated — a pantry, cellar, or cabinet. Keep potatoes in a paper bag or an open basket, never a sealed plastic bag, which traps the moisture that causes rot. Keep them away from onions: stored together they spoil each other faster, since each releases gas and moisture that ages the other.

Sort through the bag occasionally and remove any potato that has started to rot — one bad potato can soften its neighbors. And never wash potatoes before storage; washing adds surface moisture and speeds spoilage. Wash them at cooking time instead.

Varieties follow the same rules but differ slightly in stamina. Red and other waxy potatoes keep about as long as russets — 3–5 weeks, often longer in the right conditions — but their thinner skins turn soft faster if damp, so give them an especially dry, ventilated spot.

::tip
Keep potatoes in a paper bag in a dark cabinet, and take onions out of the same bag. The classic mistake is storing them together in a basket — the onion's moisture and the potato's ethylene make both spoil faster.
::

## Why potatoes sprout and turn green

Light and warmth cause both problems. Light triggers chlorophyll (the green color) and with it solanine — the bitter compound that can cause nausea in quantity. Warmth triggers sprouting. If a potato has small sprouts and only a little green, cut both away generously and cook the rest. If it's heavily green, shriveled, or tastes bitter, throw it out.

::warning
Discard any potato that is heavily green, deeply wrinkled, or tastes bitter when cooked — not just the green patches. High levels of solanine can cause stomach upset and headache. A few small sprouts trimmed off are no problem; a shriveled, bitter green potato is.
::

## Freezing potatoes

Raw potatoes are one of the few vegetables that should not go straight into the freezer — ice crystals ruin their texture and turn the flesh gray. Cook them first. Mashed, roasted, and boiled potatoes freeze well for about 3 months; so do homemade fries and hash browns. Cool cooked potatoes completely, freeze them in a single layer on a tray, then transfer to airtight bags. Thaw in the fridge and reheat until hot.

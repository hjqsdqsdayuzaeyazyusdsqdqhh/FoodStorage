Most food spoilage is not the food's fault — it's the fridge's layout. Every refrigerator has hot and cold zones, and matching food to the right zone is the difference between milk that lasts a week past its date and milk that sours in three days. This guide is the complete map.

## Step 1 — Set the temperature

The whole system rests on one number: 40°F or below for the refrigerator, 0°F or below for the freezer. The dial on the fridge is a guess, not a measurement — buy an appliance thermometer, place it in a glass of water on a middle shelf, wait 24 hours, and adjust until it reads 34–40°F. Do this once a season, because thermostats drift.

::safety
Never let food sit in the 40–140°F danger zone for more than 2 hours (1 hour if the room is above 90°F). In the danger zone, bacteria double in as little as 20 minutes. See the [food safety temperature guide](/storage-methods/food-safety/food-safety-temperature-guide/) for the full chart.
::

## Step 2 — Map the shelves

Refrigerators are colder at the bottom and back, warmer at the top and door. Work with that gradient:

- **Top shelves:** ready-to-eat food — leftovers, deli, drinks, prepared dishes. Nothing raw here.
- **Middle shelves:** dairy, eggs, yogurt, and the "use soon" spot. Cold and stable, ideal for perishables.
- **Bottom shelf:** raw meat, poultry, and seafood — the coldest zone, and the place where juices can't drip onto anything below.
- **Door shelves:** condiments, butter, drinks, jars — everything that tolerates temperature swings. Not milk. Not eggs.

## Step 3 — Use the drawers

The crisper drawers exist to control humidity. Set one drawer to high humidity for leafy greens and vegetables — high humidity keeps them crisp. Set the other to low humidity for fruit, especially ethylene emitters like apples and bananas, which last longer in drier air. If you have one drawer, wrap greens in a bag with a damp paper towel and keep fruit bagged.

Keep ethylene emitters away from ethylene-sensitive food: [apples](/shelf-life/produce/how-long-does-apples-last/) and [bananas](/shelf-life/produce/how-long-does-bananas-last/) will ripen and soften nearby greens and berries faster.

## Step 4 — Keep the door for condiments

The door swings open and shut dozens of times a day, so it's the warmest, most unstable zone. Milk, eggs, yogurt, and anything perishable that can't tolerate a 10-degree swing belongs on a stable shelf. The door earns its keep with condiments, jams, butter, and drinks — items that are shelf-stable for a while anyway.

## Step 5 — Rotate and check weekly

The fridge stays organized the same way the pantry does: [FIFO](/pantry-organization/the-fifo-pantry-system/), oldest in front, and a weekly pass. Every week, check the temperature, scan for spoilage, and pull older food forward. A ten-minute weekly round keeps the fridge from ever becoming a science experiment.

::tip
Keep a thermometer in the fridge at all times and glance at it when you put groceries away. The most common fridge failure — running too warm — announces itself in the number long before it shows up as spoiled milk.
::

## Storage times by food

Fridge life at 40°F or below: milk 5–7 days past date, eggs 3–5 weeks from pack date, hard cheese 3–4 weeks opened, raw chicken 1–2 days, ground beef 1–2 days, steak 3–5 days, lettuce 5–7 days, cooked leftovers 3–4 days. See the [shelf-life chart](/shelf-life/) for every food's exact window.

For the complete temperature rules, see the [food safety temperature guide](/storage-methods/food-safety/food-safety-temperature-guide/).

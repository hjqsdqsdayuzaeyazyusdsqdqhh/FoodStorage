Home canning is the most rewarding and the most unforgiving food preservation method. Done right, it gives you shelf-stable food for years. Done wrong — especially with low-acid foods — it can give you botulism, and that's not hyperbole. The safety rules are not negotiable, and they're also simple: use tested recipes, match the method to the food, and never improvise.

## The two methods, and the one rule

The rule that decides everything: **the acid content of the food picks the method.**

- **Boiling water bath** reaches 212°F — enough to kill bacteria in high-acid foods: fruit, jams, jellies, pickles, and tomatoes with added acid. The acid itself prevents botulism.
- **Pressure canner** reaches 240–250°F — the only method hot enough to kill Clostridium botulinum spores in low-acid foods: vegetables, meat, poultry, beans, and soups.

::safety
Never process low-acid foods in a boiling water bath. No amount of extra boiling time makes it safe — the water bath can't reach the temperature needed to kill botulism spores. Vegetables and meat require a pressure canner. This is the one canning rule with no exceptions.
::

## Step 1 — Pick the right method and recipe

Start with high-acid foods — jams, pickles, fruit — in the water bath, which is easier and lower risk. Use only tested recipes from an approved source: the USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning, university extension services, or other food-safety authorities. Never use a blog recipe, never adjust processing times, and never invent your own.

## Step 2 — Get the right equipment

You need: canning jars with new two-piece lids, a water bath canner or pressure canner, a jar lifter, a headspace tool, and a clean cloth. Tested recipes list the exact equipment; the pressure canner must be in good working order with a calibrated gauge — replace the gauge or get it tested yearly.

## Step 3 — Prep and fill jars

Clean and inspect the jars, pack the food to the recipe's specified headspace, and run a clean tool around the inside edge to remove air bubbles. Wipe the jar rims with a clean, damp cloth — any food on the rim breaks the seal — then place the lids and screw bands fingertip-tight, not cranked.

## Step 4 — Process for the exact time

Follow the recipe's processing time exactly. In a water bath, the water must return to a full, rolling boil before the timer starts, and stay boiling for the whole time. In a pressure canner, hold the correct pressure for the full time, then let the canner cool naturally — never force-cool it.

::tip
Start with the recipe's altitude adjustment. Processing times change above 1,000 feet elevation because water boils at a lower temperature. Tested recipes include altitude tables — follow them, or you're processing at a lower temperature than the recipe assumes.
::

## Step 5 — Check seals and label

After cooling 12–24 hours, press the center of each lid: a properly sealed lid is slightly concave and does not flex. Remove the bands, label each jar with the contents and date, and store in a cool, dark place below 75°F. Home-canned food is best within a year and safe for up to two.

## How to spot a failed jar

Before opening: a bulging lid, a loose lid, or liquid leaking from the seal means the jar failed — discard it without tasting. After opening: off-odor, mold, foaming, or spurting liquid means discard. For low-acid food stored more than a year, the cautious rule is: when in doubt, throw it out.

## Where canning fits

Canning is the longest-term method for shelf-stable food — the home equivalent of the [canned food](/shelf-life/pantry/how-long-does-canned-food-last/) in your pantry. For the temperature rules that govern it, see the [food safety temperature guide](/storage-methods/food-safety/food-safety-temperature-guide/).

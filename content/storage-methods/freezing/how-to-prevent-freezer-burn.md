Freezer burn looks like the freezer turned your food into a science experiment, but it's much simpler: it's dehydration. Cold, dry freezer air pulls moisture from the food's surface, leaving pale, leathery patches. It's safe — but it tastes like the freezer, and it's entirely preventable with one idea: air must never touch frozen food.

## What freezer burn actually is

Every freezer is cold and dry, and dry air wants moisture. When air touches frozen food, moisture evaporates from the surface and the exposed area dehydrates — turning gray, dry, and tough. The process takes time, so freezer burn is the signature of long storage or sloppy wrapping. Ice crystals can also grow large over time and puncture cell walls, which is why slow-thawed food goes mushy.

The crucial fact: freezer burn is not spoilage. Freezing stops bacteria, so burned food is safe to eat. It just tastes flat and feels dry — which is why it's a quality problem with a packaging solution.

## Step 1 — Remove the air

The single rule of freezer storage: no air on the food. Three ways to do it:

1. **Vacuum sealer** — removes all air; the gold standard, roughly doubles quality life.
2. **Freezer bags** — press every bit of air out before sealing; use the water-displacement trick for best results.
3. **Plastic wrap** — wrap tightly against the food, pressing out the air as you go.

::tip
To press air out of a freezer bag, fill it, seal all but a corner, and dip it in a bowl of water — the water pressure pushes the air out. Seal the corner and you've got a near-vacuum seal without a machine.
::

## Step 2 — Use multiple layers

For anything frozen more than a month, double up. Wrap meat in plastic wrap pressed tight, then add a layer of foil or slide it into a freezer bag. Two layers mean even if the outer layer gets nicked, the inner seal still protects the food. Vacuum-sealed packages usually need no extra layer.

## Step 3 — Freeze fast and cold

Freeze at 0°F or below, in the coldest, most open spot in the freezer, and keep the door closed as much as possible. Fast freezing forms small ice crystals; slow freezing forms large ones that puncture cell walls and turn food mushy. Don't stack fresh packages on top of food you're thawing — the warm air slows freezing.

## Step 4 — Rotate and use

Freezer burn is a time problem, so rotation is the last line of defense. Label everything with the date, use the oldest first ([FIFO](/pantry-organization/the-fifo-pantry-system/)), and don't keep food frozen past its quality window. The [frozen food shelf-life chart](/shelf-life/freezer/) tells you how long each food keeps its best quality.

## Step 5 — Trim and cook around it

If freezer burn has already happened, trim the dry, gray patches before cooking — they're tough and flavorless. Use the rest in moist dishes: stews, soups, braises, and sauces where any remaining dryness goes unnoticed. Badly burned food is safe but bland; season boldly or use it where texture doesn't matter.

## The bottom line

Freezer burn is the only enemy in a 0°F freezer, and it's defeated by one habit: seal out the air. Vacuum-seal or press-wrapped, frozen fast, labeled and rotated, food in your freezer stays at its peak instead of turning gray.

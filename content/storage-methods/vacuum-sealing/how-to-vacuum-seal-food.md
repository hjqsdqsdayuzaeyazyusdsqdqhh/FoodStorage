Vacuum sealing is the difference between a freezer that preserves and a freezer that burns. Removing air stops the two enemies of frozen food — freezer burn and oxidation — which is why sealed food keeps its best quality about twice as long. Buy a sealer, learn the five steps, and your freezer becomes the best storage room in the house.

## What vacuum sealing actually does

A vacuum sealer sucks the air out of a bag and seals it airtight. No air means no oxygen to oxidize flavor and no moisture reservoir to create freezer burn. For the same food, vacuum-sealed and frozen at 0°F: raw meat keeps best quality 12–18 months instead of 6–9; cheese months longer; coffee, nuts, and dry goods lose the rancidity clock entirely.

The honest caveat: vacuum sealing does not make food safe from bacteria. It removes oxygen, not contamination — and some dangerous bacteria grow without oxygen. Seal food that's fresh, and follow the same fridge and freezer rules you always would.

## Step 1 — Choose the right food

The winners: raw meat, poultry, and fish; cheese; coffee beans; nuts; dry goods; and cooked leftovers for long freezer life. The tricky: soups, stews, and anything liquid (pre-freeze them first); soft foods like berries and bread (they crush); and sharp-edged items like pasta and bones (they can puncture the bag — wrap them first).

## Step 2 — Prep and portion

Trim and portion into meal sizes so you thaw only what you need. Pat meat and fish dry before sealing — surface moisture interferes with the seal. For raw meat, some cooks add a marinate or season before sealing; just keep the seal area clean and dry.

## Step 3 — Load the bag

Lay the food in the bag and leave 2–3 inches of clear space at the open end. That clear space is where the vacuum and the seal happen. If the seal area gets juice, grease, or crumbs on it, the bag will not seal airtight — wipe the interior of the open end before closing.

::tip
Put a folded paper towel in the bag corner before sealing raw meat. It soaks up the juices that the vacuum would otherwise pull toward the seal, which is the most common cause of bags that won't seal.
::

## Step 4 — Seal with the machine

Close the lid, run the vacuum cycle, and watch the seal bar. When it's done, test: the bag should be tight, with the food visibly compressed and no air pockets. If a bag fails to seal, wipe the seal area dry and run it again, or trim the bag and reseal.

## Step 5 — Label, freeze, and rotate

Label every bag with the contents and the date — unlabeled vacuum bags become identical mysteries in a month. Freeze flat at 0°F so bags stack like books and food freezes fast. Rotate [FIFO](/pantry-organization/the-fifo-pantry-system/) so the oldest gets used first, and follow the [frozen food shelf-life chart](/shelf-life/freezer/) for each food's quality window.

## What to vacuum seal vs. leave in the bag

- **Seal:** meat, poultry, fish, cheese, coffee, nuts, dry goods, pre-frozen liquids.
- **Pre-freeze then seal:** soups, stews, marinades, anything that pours.
- **Leave alone:** berries and soft fruit (freeze in a tray first, then seal), bread (crush risk), anything you'll eat this week (a sealed bag costs more than a zip-top bag).

## Is it worth it?

If you buy meat in bulk, freeze seasonal produce, or keep coffee and cheese long-term, a vacuum sealer pays for itself within months — in food not thrown away and quality not lost. If you mostly cook fresh and eat everything within the week, it's an optional upgrade. When it fits your routine, it's the closest thing to a time machine in the kitchen.
